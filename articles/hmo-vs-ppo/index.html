HMO vs PPO: Which Rules Can You Live With?

Compare HMO and PPO networks, specialist referrals, prior approval and total costs. Four practical questions to ask before choosing your health plan.

The quick comparison

It’s late, the benefits portal is open, and you’re supposed to choose between HMO and PPO. Two sets of letters, one deadline, and absolutely nobody making this fun.

This is the companion to the Paul On Money HMO vs PPO video. [1]

Words to know

  • HMO (health maintenance organization): a plan type that usually covers care only from doctors and hospitals in its network, except in an emergency.
  • PPO (preferred provider organization): a plan type with a network where you pay less, but you can also go outside it if you pay more.
  • Network: the doctors, hospitals and other providers your insurance plan has contracted with to give care.
  • Premium: the amount you pay for your health insurance every month, whether or not you use any care.
  • Deductible: what you pay for covered care yourself before your insurance starts to pay its share.
  • Out-of-pocket maximum: the most you pay for covered services in a plan year, with some costs not counting toward it.

A referral and insurer approval are different things

A referral is an order from your primary care doctor to see a specialist or get certain services. Many HMOs require one, and skipping it may mean the plan will not pay. “Many” matters: check your own plan instead of treating the label as the complete rulebook. [3]

Prior authorization is a decision by the insurance company or plan about whether certain care is medically necessary. It is separate from your doctor’s referral, and it is not a guarantee that every cost will be covered. A plan can require authorization even when you do not need a specialist referral. [4]

So “I don’t need a referral” does not mean “there is no paperwork.” Insurance would never give up that easily.

Same sore knee, different route

Here’s a hypothetical example, like Sam’s in the video.

Sam needs a knee specialist. Under an HMO that requires referrals, she starts with her primary care doctor and follows the plan’s specialist process. Under a PPO, she may have a more direct route, including an out-of-network option at added cost. Neither route tells her whether an MRI needs insurer approval. That is another question to check. [2] [3] [4]

The useful comparison is not “freedom versus bad insurance.” It is whether the route to the care you need works for you, and what that route costs.

Four questions before you choose

1. Is my actual doctor in the exact network?

Check the plan’s directory, then confirm with the doctor’s office. Give them the full plan and network name. “We take that insurance company” does not settle whether they participate in the particular option on your enrollment screen.

Include the hospital, specialists and other providers you already know you need. If keeping a particular doctor matters, settle that before comparing small differences in premiums. This is a practical verification checklist, not a promise that a directory will remain unchanged.

2. What must happen before a specialist visit or procedure?

Write down the referral requirement and the prior-authorization requirement separately. Ask who submits each request and how you confirm it was received. A primary care appointment and an insurer approval are not interchangeable steps.

3. What happens outside the network?

Ask about routine visits, travel, emergencies and ambulance transport separately. Do not treat emergency exceptions as permission to book ordinary out-of-network care.

Federal surprise-billing protections cover many emergency situations for people using employer or individual health insurance. They do not make care free, and ground ambulances generally fall outside the federal No Surprises Act protections. State protections may differ. [5]

4. What does the whole year cost?

Compare your annual premiums with your share of the care you expect. Then look at a more expensive year and whether you could handle an early bill. The deductible is part of your medical spending, not an extra amount to add a second time. [6]

Use this starting point:

Annual premiums + your estimated share of care = a starting annual cost estimate.

Keep noncovered care and out-of-network costs visible rather than assuming they follow the in-network rules. Check the out-of-pocket limit’s exclusions in your documents.

There is no universal winner from the letters alone. A lower premium does not settle whether the plan is cheaper for the care you need.

Put the documents next to each other

Ask benefits for the Summary of Benefits and Coverage, the short comparison document, plus the fuller plan documents. Obtain your employee premium from the enrollment material if it is not included in the summary. [7]

My take

An HMO may work well if the needed doctors are in its network and its care process fits you. A PPO may be worth considering if access outside the network matters enough to justify the additional cost. That is a way to use the facts, not a recommendation based on three letters.

Care you cannot skip, eligibility for the plan and what you can afford come first. Preference about paperwork breaks the tie.

Pick the rules you can live with. Then check the actual price of living with them.
